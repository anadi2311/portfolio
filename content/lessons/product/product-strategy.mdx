[Lesson 2](/learn/product/business-objective) got you to a funded case: objective, beachhead, personas, ask. Strategy takes the next step. You choose where to play, how to win, and what you will refuse while everyone else asks for more.

Most teams do not fail because they lack ideas. They fail because they carry too many. The roadmap grows, the customer gets blurry, and the product turns into a compromise machine.

Richard Rumelt's [*Good Strategy Bad Strategy*](https://www.goodstrategybadstrategy.com/), Roger Martin and A.G. Lafley's [*Playing to Win*](https://www.rogerlmartin.com/books/playing-to-win), Michael Porter's [What Is Strategy?](https://hbr.org/1996/11/what-is-strategy), and Hamilton Helmer's [*7 Powers*](https://www.7powers.com/) all press the same point from different angles: strategy is choice under constraint. It narrows the field.

## Strategy narrows the business case

You already wrote the case in Lesson 2. That case named the market, the beachhead, and the metric. Strategy turns that into a smaller set of choices the team can hold onto when requests pile up.

Martin and Lafley give the cleanest PM version: **where to play** and **how to win**. Porter pushes the same discipline from a different direction: fit your activities together so rivals cannot copy one feature and catch you. Rumelt asks for a diagnosis, a guiding policy, and coherent actions. Different words. Same job. You cut options so the product can matter to someone specific.

### Netflix

**Netflix**, mid-2000s: Reed Hastings did not pursue every entertainment format at once. The company picked streaming and accepted the operational and licensing pain that came with it. The move looked messy in public, especially around Qwikster, but the strategic choice was clear: own the digital habit before the DVD business ran out of road. The lesson for you is not "be bold." The lesson is that strategy forces a sequence and makes some revenue lines look less important for a while.

### Southwest

**Southwest** did the same in airlines. Porter's classic fit example still holds: point-to-point routes, fast turns, no elaborate meal service, no hub complexity. Each choice reinforced the others. Competitors copied pieces and still lost the economics because they kept the old system.

Strategy starts paying off when the roadmap gets shorter.

## Where to play, how to win, what to refuse

PMs often stop at "who is the customer?" Strategy asks four questions after that.

1. **Where will you play first?** Segment, geography, product surface, price band.
2. **How will you win there?** Speed, trust, density, workflow fit, data, cost, brand.
3. **How will you capture them?** Motion, first channel, wedge, whole product for that niche.

Then you write the part teams avoid:

4. **What will you refuse for now?**

Geoffrey Moore's [*Crossing the Chasm*](https://www.geoffreyamoore.com/book/crossing-the-chasm-3rd-edition/) helps on the first question. Pick one beachhead tight enough that references compound. Helmer helps on the second. Balfour and the GTM section below help on the third. Porter and Rumelt help on the fourth. Refusal keeps the system coherent.

## Who you play first: user, buyer, beachhead

"Customer" is a fog word. Strategy needs three names before "where to play first" means anything.

### Three names

| Name | Question | If you skip it |
|---|---|---|
| **User** | Who does the job in their week? | You ship for a title, not a situation |
| **Buyer** | Who can say yes (budget, time, or adoption)? | You delight a user who cannot start |
| **Beachhead** | Which *pair* (or same person) will you dominate first? | You chase the whole TAM |

Moore's chasm work is about **first customers who can reference each other**: a niche small enough to win, specific enough that word of mouth works. Lesson 2 used that for the business case. Here you use it to force a play: one user, one buyer, one first pin.

In B2B the user and the buyer are often different people. Salesforce's early buyer was the VP who owned pipeline; the user was the rep living in the CRM. Slack's early user was the team in the channel; the buyer showed up later as IT or a department lead when the tool had already spread. PayPal's beachhead user was the eBay power seller; the "buyer" was the same person paying fees because the pain was theirs.

In consumer and PLG, user and buyer can be the same person. That is still a choice you write down, not an assumption you hide.

### How you decide

1. **Name the job**, not the demographic. Christensen's [Jobs to Be Done](https://hbr.org/2016/09/know-your-customers-jobs-to-be-done) keeps you in the situation. "PM who fears the eng review" is a job. "All product people 25–40" is a slide.
2. **Split user and buyer.** If they differ, say who feels the pain and who can start without a committee. If they are the same, write "user = buyer" so nobody invents a procurement motion later.
3. **Pick the beachhead as a pin you can dominate.** Density, references, a whole product they can say yes to. Moore's test: can this niche talk to itself?
4. **Name pin two.** Adjacent, not "everyone else." Engineers on the same site are pin two for this product. A second homepage is a different strategy.

### This app

**This app:** the **beachhead user** is the PM without engineering depth who needs stack literacy — eng reviews, timelines, small-team MVPs and POCs, staying relevant when AI drafts code. There is **no buyer in v1**. Time is what they spend. Free PM lessons are the **trust and showcase layer** (portfolio proof + free education). Free tech lessons are the **product wedge**. A later packaging buyer might be the same person. Until demand funds packaging, do not write GTM as if a sales team exists. Pin two is the engineer learning PM on the same home page, after pin one is referenceable.

Good PMs can say, in one paragraph, who uses, who starts, which pin is first, and which tempting audience waits.

### Slack

**Slack** gives you a useful example. Early Slack won inside teams that already felt the pain of fragmented internal communication. It did not start as a universal communication platform for every company workflow. The product got strong in one behavior loop first: team communication that people used all day. That constraint shaped integrations, notifications, search, and onboarding.

## Strategy is sequence, not a slogan

Teams love mission language because nobody has to say no inside it. "Delight customers." "Power creativity." "Build the future of work." Those lines belong in brand copy. Strategy needs a sequence.

Ben Thompson's [Stratechery](https://stratechery.com/) is useful here because he keeps asking where value accumulates and why one layer wins over another. That habit matters for product strategy. You do not win every layer at once. You pick the layer you can dominate and sequence the rest around it.

### Amazon

**Amazon**, 1995-1997: books first. That was not a tiny ambition. It was a tight opening. Huge catalog, familiar buying behavior, obvious online advantage. The strategy came through the order: selection in one category, then trust, logistics, and category expansion. Lesson 2 used Amazon as a beachhead example. Lesson 3 uses it to show sequence.

### Shopify

**Shopify** did something similar in commerce software. The company served merchants who wanted control without a custom engineering team. That choice shaped product simplicity, app ecosystem decisions, channel work, and pricing. Enterprise complexity came later.

If your strategy sentence does not imply an order of operations, you still have a wish list.

## Strategy for this app

Use this site as the specimen.

The business objective: earn demand from PMs who need technical depth, taught on a live product — with a free PM spine for trust and showcase, free tech lessons as the PLG wedge, and packaging only if demand earns it.

That objective still leaves too much open. Strategy makes it sharper.

### Where we will play

- **Pin one (beachhead):** PMs without engineering depth who need to understand how the stack works (reviews, timelines, MVPs/POCs, small teams, AI-era relevance)
- Free PM lessons as credibility and free education (dual purpose: teach + showcase the author's PM craft)
- Free tech lessons as the product experience that sells the next step
- Second entry later: engineers learning PM craft from the same home page
- Public web first, not cohort software, not enterprise training

### How we will win

- Teach tech and PM method on the product in front of the reader
- PLG: free lessons do the selling; a few free tech lessons prove the job; paid depth only after demand ([Product-led growth](/learn/product/product-led-growth) goes deeper)
- Two scoreboards on purpose: **product** (tech interest, waitlist, free-tech completion) and **personal** (portfolio + free teaching continues even if demand is thin — hosting cost is low)
- Readable, cited lessons so the site earns trust

### What we will refuse for now

- No login wall on free lessons
- No Stripe until a funded packaging hypothesis (price discovery lives in the pricing lesson)
- No course platform sprawl: certificates, cohorts, dashboards, community-as-growth
- No ads or SDR motion as the plan
- No pretending the free PM spine alone is the product bet

That refusal list is the strategy doing work. It protects the product from accidental complexity.

### Positioning

April Dunford's [*Obviously Awesome*](https://www.aprildunford.com/obviously-awesome) is the PM book for this line: you do not invent a category in a workshop. You name the **competitive alternatives** the beachhead already uses, then say why you win for that job.

For this site the alternatives are slide-deck PM courses, engineering blogs that never teach for PMs, and bootcamps that skip the live product. The position: **you learn how tech works on the product in front of you — free PM craft builds trust; free tech lessons are the wedge.** If a reader cannot repeat that, the GTM copy is still a slogan.

### GTM: how we capture the beachhead

Product strategy without GTM is a customer you never reach. GTM strategy is the capture plan: motion, first channel, wedge, and what stays out until the beachhead is won.

Brian Balfour's [four fits](https://brianbalfour.com/essays/product-market-fit-isnt-enough) is a useful check: product, market, channel, and model have to fit each other. Free public lessons are a **product-led** motion. Paid ads, outbound, and a sales team would be a different company. Wes Bush's [*Product Led Growth*](https://www.productled.com/product-led-growth-book) and the later [PLG lesson](/learn/product/product-led-growth) cover free forever vs free-to-paid. This lesson only names the motion so the product and the channel do not fight.

**Dropbox** captured early users with a demo video and a waitlist. **Slack** spread inside companies because one team could adopt without a procurement cycle. **This site:** capture is public URL, LinkedIn / Lenny-style PM communities / meetups, portfolio credibility (`/about`), and an **honest tech waitlist before the free tech curriculum**. Free tech lesson starts join the scoreboard after the first lessons ship. No paid acquisition, no SDR.

Write these four lines for any product:

| GTM choice | This app |
|---|---|
| **Motion** | Product-led: free lessons sell the next step |
| **First channel** | Public web + founder network (LinkedIn, PM Slack/communities, meetups) |
| **Wedge** | Honest tech waitlist first; free tech lessons after demand; waitlist / later tech completion as signal |
| **Stay out for now** | Ads, sales, login wall, certificates, community as a growth hack |

Moore's **whole product** sits here too. Early: free PM spine for trust + a credible waitlist path for tech. After signal: readable free tech lessons on the live specimen. A waitlist with no plan to ship lessons is a fake door. A twelve-lesson eng dump before demand is theater.

Bowling-pin capture: win PMs who need tech depth first. Engineers learning PM on the same home page are pin two. Do not split into two sites to look bigger.

Marty Cagan's strategy writing often pushes teams toward value, differentiation, and market understanding. Useful. The missing move in many teams is still refusal. Your roadmap starts behaving once you write the no list in plain language. The GTM list is the same discipline applied to distribution.

### Dual purpose (when demand is thin)

If the product demand signal is near zero, a pure PLG company stops or pivots. This site has a second scoreboard: the author keeps building free lessons to teach and to show tech-PM craft. That choice is explicit. It does not invent fake PMF. Packaging still waits on demand. [Product-led growth](/learn/product/product-led-growth) separates free-as-funnel from free-as-the-product.

## A strategy memo a PM can defend

You do not need a fifty-slide deck. You need a short memo the team can quote when trade-offs hit.

Use this shape:

| Section | What you write |
|---|---|
| **Diagnosis** | What constraint or market fact forces choice |
| **Where to play** | User, buyer, first beachhead pin |
| **How to win** | The edge that makes you preferable there |
| **Positioning** | Alternatives they use today, and why you win that job |
| **GTM** | Motion, first channel, wedge, whole product for the niche |
| **Refusals** | What stays out of product and of distribution |
| **Sequence** | First pin, second pin, later |
| **Metric** | What would prove capture is working |

### The metric must prove the edge

A strategy metric should test the causal claim inside "how to win." Aggregate traffic cannot prove that a chosen beachhead prefers the claimed advantage.

For instance, consider Slack's team adoption model. If the strategy claims that workflow fit helps small software teams replace fragmented communication, total account creation is weak evidence. A sharper chain would track:

1. Target software teams create a workspace.
2. Several teammates complete the first shared workflow.
3. The team repeats that workflow in later weeks.

Suppose 100 target teams create a workspace, 60 complete the chosen activation behavior, and 30 remain active four weeks later. Those numbers show where capture weakens. Another 1,000 signups from unrelated segments may increase the company total while hiding failure in the beachhead.

Write the metric with the segment and behavior attached: "four-week retained target teams that completed the shared workflow." A bare "monthly active users grow" target drops the beachhead and the claimed edge. [Product-led growth](/learn/product/product-led-growth) defines activation; [Product analytics and GTM](/learn/product/product-analytics-gtm) explains retention cohorts and channel quality.

**Apple**, 2007-2010: iPhone did not open with every carrier, every app model, every price tier, and every enterprise feature. Apple picked a narrow stack it could control, then expanded. You can disagree with the specific choices. You cannot miss the strategic discipline.

The memo matters because stakeholder pressure arrives one request at a time. Sales wants a checkbox. Design wants polish time. Engineering wants a platform cleanup. The CEO wants a headline. Strategy gives you a reasoned basis for yes, no, and later.

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | What is happening |
|---|---|
| "We serve everyone" | No beachhead; no sequence |
| "The customer is enterprises" | User and buyer never named |
| "Our strategy is innovation" | Word without a customer or edge |
| "We'll figure out GTM later" | Product with no capture path |
| Roadmap full of custom asks | Refusals missing |
| Metric does not match the edge | Strategy and scoreboard pulled apart |
| Team changes direction every meeting | Strategy never got written in a form people can quote |

Good PMs measure themselves by whether the product gets more coherent over time. Bad PMs call every request an opportunity and hand engineering a bag of contradictions.

## Strategy before experiments

Experiments come next, but strategy has to narrow the field first.

If your strategy says this app wins by teaching tech depth to PMs on a live product, your experiments should test that claim:

- Do beachhead PMs join a tech waitlist from non-friend channels?
- Does founder outreach (LinkedIn, PM communities, meetups) bring non-friend waitlist signups?
- After the first free tech lessons ship, do waitlist members start and finish them?
- Does the free PM spine still build trust without being mistaken for the whole product bet?

Those are strategic hypotheses. The next lesson turns them into concrete tests with a metric and a stop rule.

## Before you go on

Pick a product you know. Write:

1. The **user** (job in their week) is ___.
2. The **buyer** (who can start) is ___ (or write "same as user").
3. The first beachhead pin is ___.
4. We will win because ___.
5. They find us through ___ (motion + first channel).
6. The wedge / first value is ___.
7. We will refuse ___ in the product and ___ in GTM.
8. We will do ___ first and ___ later.
9. We will know capture is working when ___ moves.

If you cannot fill lines 1–3, you do not know who you play first.
