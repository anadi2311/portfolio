[Lesson 2](/learn/product/business-objective) left price as a sketch. [Lesson 7](/learn/product/product-led-growth) separated free forever from free-to-paid. This lesson is the **pricing case**: willingness to pay, packaging, structure (one-time vs recurring), and unit economics you can defend.

Price is not a spreadsheet afterthought. <Key>Price is a hypothesis about value.</Key> You test it like any other bet.

## Price follows value (not cost, not vibes)

Three common logics. Only one should lead.

| Logic | Question | Failure mode |
|---|---|---|
| **Cost-plus** | What did it cost us + margin? | Ignores what the buyer values |
| **Competition** | What do peers charge? | Copies someone else's beachhead |
| **Value-based** | What is the outcome worth to this buyer? | Needs research; worth it |

Madhavan Ramanujam and Georg Tacke's [*Monetizing Innovation*](https://www.simon-kucher.com/en/who-we-are/experts/madhavan-ramanujam) is the modern product book: design the **offer and price** before you finish the product. Feature factories price last and then wonder why conversion is flat.

Thomas Nagle, John Hogan, and Joseph Zale's [*The Strategy and Tactics of Pricing*](https://www.routledge.com/The-Strategy-and-Tactics-of-Pricing/Nagle-Hogan-Zale/p/book/9781032178657) is the classic: segmentation, value communication, price structure, and why "average cost" pricing destroys margin.

Hermann Simon's [*Confessions of the Pricing Man*](https://www.confessionsofthepricingman.com/) shows how companies make pricing decisions. William Poundstone's [*Priceless*](https://www.williampoundstone.net/Priceless.html) covers behavioral traps (anchors, decoys). Rafi Mohammed's [*The Art of Pricing*](https://www.artofpricing.com/) is practical field craft for product and sales teams.

Read them as a spine. Do not quote them as wallpaper on a pricing slide.

## Willingness to pay (WTP)

WTP is a range, not a single number. You learn it with cheap tests before Stripe.

| Method | What you learn | Watch out |
|---|---|---|
| **Problem interviews** | Whether the job is worth money at all | Leading with "would you pay $X?" |
| **Van Westendorp** | Too cheap / bargain / expensive / too expensive bands | Needs enough respondents; not gospel |
| **Purchase intent / fake door** | Behavior toward a stated price; conversation alone cannot show purchase intent | Honesty gap |
| **Conjoint / tradeoff** | Which features pull WTP | Expensive; earn it later |
| **Live price test** | Real conversion | Needs traffic and ethics |

Van Westendorp (price sensitivity meter) is the chart most PMs meet first: four questions that sketch a band. Use it as a **map**, then validate with behavior.

## Packaging and structure

Packaging answers: what is free, what is paid, what is bundled.

Structure answers: how money moves over time.

| Structure | Fits when | Weak when |
|---|---|---|
| **One-time / long-lived access** | Durable knowledge or a finished artifact; buyer wants ownership | You owe continuous service that grows cost with usage |
| **Subscription** | Ongoing value, updates, hosting tied to active use, seat expansion | The product is a book of knowledge people finish once |
| **Usage** | Cost and value track consumption (API calls) | Unpredictable bills scare the beachhead |
| **Seats** | Value scales with people in an org | Solo buyer; seat games |

**Adobe Creative Cloud** moved from boxed software (one-time) to subscription because the value became continuous cloud + updates. **Basecamp** kept simple, opinionated pricing instead of enterprise maze pricing. Different jobs, different structures.

For a **knowledge product** (lessons, methods, reference), one-time long-lived access often matches the job better than monthly: the buyer pays when they commit to learning, then returns for years without a meter on every reread. Monthly billing fits Netflix-style ongoing entertainment or SaaS that dies when you cancel. It fights "I bought a book of craft."

## Unit economics (the case must close)

Before you publish a price, write the math:

1. **Price** (what the buyer pays)
2. **Variable cost per buyer** (payments fees, support, bandwidth)
3. **Contribution** = price − variable cost
4. **Fixed cost to cover** (hosting, domain, tools for N years)
5. **Break-even buyers** = fixed cost / contribution
6. **Demand hypothesis** = how many beachhead buyers in the planning window

If break-even needs a fantasy funnel, the price or the cost is wrong. Change one of them. Do not invent TAM.

### When acquisition starts: cost, lifetime value, and payback

For instance, consider a subscription product charging $30 per month. Each paying customer creates $6 per month in payment, support, and usage costs.

- **Revenue** is the $30 collected before costs.
- **Contribution dollars** are the money left after variable costs: `$30 - $6 = $24` per customer per month. This money can cover fixed costs and profit.
- **Contribution margin** is contribution divided by revenue: `$24 ÷ $30 = 80%`. The percentage makes products with different prices easier to compare.

Now add acquisition. **Customer acquisition cost (CAC)** measures how much sales and marketing spend the company used to acquire each new paying customer:

```text
CAC = sales and marketing spend ÷ new paying customers
```

If one channel costs $36,000 during a quarter and brings 300 new paying customers, CAC is `$36,000 ÷ 300 = $120`. Keep the spend, customers, channel, and time period aligned. Blending a cheap referral channel with an expensive advertising channel hides where the company loses money.

**Customer lifetime value (LTV)** estimates the contribution a customer will generate before leaving:

```text
LTV = contribution per period × expected paid lifetime
```

If customers contribute $24 per month and stay for an estimated 18 months, estimated LTV is `$24 × 18 = $432`. This is an assumption until enough customer cohorts have completed a meaningful part of their lifetime. Calling $540 of lifetime revenue "LTV" would ignore the costs required to serve that customer.

Two comparisons turn those numbers into decisions:

- **CAC payback** asks how long contribution takes to recover acquisition cost: `$120 ÷ $24 = 5 months`. A long payback consumes cash before the company earns its spend back.
- **LTV:CAC** compares expected lifetime contribution with acquisition cost: `$432 ÷ $120 = 3.6`. The ratio is useful only when both numbers use the same customer segment and honest cost assumptions.

Product stage controls which number deserves trust:

- **Before paid acquisition:** test willingness to pay, pricing comprehension, and purchase intent. You cannot measure observed CAC until the company spends money on acquisition and gains paying customers.
- **Early paid product:** measure conversion, contribution per purchase, refunds, support cost, and CAC by channel. Treat LTV as a range built from retention assumptions.
- **After repeat behavior appears:** estimate LTV from customer cohorts, update it as more customers renew or leave, and compare it with CAC and payback.

A pricing experiment should measure economic value rather than celebrate conversion alone. Suppose a $20 offer converts 10% of eligible visitors and leaves $15 contribution per purchase. It creates `$1.50` contribution per eligible visitor. A $30 offer converts 8% and leaves $25 contribution, creating `$2.00` per eligible visitor. Conversion fell, but the second offer funds more value per opportunity. Refunds, later churn, support contacts, and trust remain guardrails.

## Specimen: pricing paid tech depth on this site

### Strategy constraints (from Lesson 3)

- Free PM spine stays free (trust + showcase)
- Free tech wedge / waitlist first; paid depth only after demand
- No login wall on free lessons
- Dual scoreboard: teaching may continue even if paid demand is thin

### The offer we price

**Paid:** long-lived access to the **product engineering** depth (structured tech-for-PMs curriculum beyond the free wedge).

**Not sold monthly.** Knowledge is closer to a book than to a streaming feed. Pay once when the value is clear; keep access for a decade while the site stays up.

**Access window:** **10 years** from purchase (assumption: we keep the portfolio hosted that long; renew the promise in plain language if hosting reality changes).

### Why not $10/month

| Monthly subscription | One-time long-lived |
|---|---|
| Implies ongoing drip of new product every month | Matches "I learn the craft; I return when I need it" |
| Feels like SaaS tax on knowledge | Feels like buying a durable reference |
| Churn anxiety for both sides | Clear break-even math on hosting |
| Fights PLG trust on a teaching site | Aligns with free forever PM + paid depth |

Monthly can be right for cohorts, live office hours, or a community that costs every month to run. That is a different offer. This specimen is **curriculum access**, not a membership club.

### Price hypothesis: **$49 one-time** (10-year access)

Not $10 forever-access if we want hosting covered without heroic volume. Not $199 before waitlist proof. **$49** sits in the band we already floated on the waitlist WTP probe (~$50) and clears a sober cost case.

Illustrative economics (assumptions; update when real costs land):

| Line | Assumption |
|---|---|
| Hosting + domain + basic tools | **$20 / month** → **$2,400 over 10 years** |
| Stripe-ish fee | ~3% + $0.30 → ~**$1.80** per $49 sale |
| Support / refunds reserve | **$3** per buyer (assumption) |
| Variable cost per buyer | ~**$5** |
| Contribution per buyer | $49 − $5 = **$44** |
| Break-even buyers (hosting only) | $2,400 / $44 ≈ **55 buyers** |
| Demand hypothesis | From waitlist: if **400** non-friend waitlist joins and **15%** convert → **60** buyers → covers 10-year hosting with a thin cushion |

If conversion is 10% you need ~550 waitlist joins or a higher price. If hosting is $10/month, break-even drops. **Write the real numbers when you have invoices.** The method stays: fixed cost ÷ contribution = buyers you must earn.

$19 one-time is possible as a "tip jar" price; break-even jumps to ~140+ buyers at the same costs. $79 raises contribution and lowers break-even if WTP holds. Start with the waitlist WTP question, then A/B only when traffic is honest ([Lesson 8](/learn/product/product-analytics-gtm)).

### Case narrative (eight lines)

1. **Buyer:** PM without eng depth who finished free trust lessons and wants structured tech depth.
2. **Job:** stack literacy for reviews, timelines, MVPs/POCs.
3. **Offer:** one-time $49 for ~10 years of paid tech curriculum access.
4. **Why not monthly:** knowledge product; ownership over meter.
5. **Cost case:** ~$2,400 hosting over 10 years; ~55 buyers at $49 cover it.
6. **Demand gate:** waitlist + WTP probe before Stripe ([Lesson 4](/learn/product/experiments)).
7. **Guardrails:** free PM spine stays free; no dark patterns on the waitlist.
8. **Kill:** if non-friend WTP is near zero, keep free teaching; do not invent a price from vanity clicks.

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | What is happening |
|---|---|
| Price the product after build | Monetizing Innovation failure mode |
| Cost-plus only | Buyer does not care about your AWS bill |
| Monthly by default | Structure fights the job (knowledge vs stream) |
| $0 or $999 with no WTP | Avoidance or vanity |
| Ignore payment fees and support | Fake contribution margin |
| Use lifetime revenue as LTV | Serving costs disappear from the calculation |
| Blend every channel into one CAC | An expensive channel hides behind referrals or organic traffic |
| Extrapolate years of LTV from one month | A fragile retention assumption looks like observed value |
| Compare CAC and LTV from different segments or periods | The ratio combines customers with different economics |
| Paywall the free trust layer | Breaks Lesson 3 refusals |

## Before you go on

1. Write the **value** in one sentence for your beachhead buyer.
2. Pick **structure**: one-time, subscription, usage, or seats, and why.
3. Draft a **Van Westendorp or waitlist WTP** question (no live charge yet).
4. Fill the **unit economics** six lines (price, variable cost, contribution, fixed cost, break-even, demand hypothesis).
5. Name what stays **free** so trust does not collapse.
6. If paid acquisition exists, calculate contribution margin and CAC by channel. Estimate LTV and payback with the assumptions written beside them.
