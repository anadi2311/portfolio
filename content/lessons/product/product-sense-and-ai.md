Generative AI did not rewrite the PM job. It rewrote the **Tuesday**. Specs arrive in minutes. Interview notes collapse into themes before lunch. Prototypes multiply while the coffee is still hot.

The job from [lesson 1](/learn/product/how-pm-begins) is unchanged: **make the product successful on terms the company can measure.** AI is a new tool in the kit. Product sense and taste are how you use it without fooling yourself.

## Tools commoditize. Judgment does not.

Lenny Rachitsky's [How AI will impact product management](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/how-ai-will-impact-product-management) ranks what shifts: drafting specs, summarizing research, roadmap scaffolding, and parts of discovery get heavy model assistance. Strategy, stakeholder trust, picking the problem, and killing a bad bet stay human.

Shreyas Doshi states the long-term logic in [Why Product Sense is the only product skill that will matter in the AI age](https://shreyasdoshi.substack.com/p/why-product-sense-is-the-only-product): when every team runs similar models, which chatbot you use stops being an edge. Your edge is how you **improve on** what the model returns. The insight from three customer calls the model never had. The no that saves a quarter. The prioritization that fits your runway, not a generic benchmark answer.

He labels that bundle **product sense**: the ability to make correct macro and micro product decisions under ambiguity. Five skills compose it:

| Skill | Plain English | AI can help with… | You still own… |
|---|---|---|---|
| Empathy | Why users act the way they do | Interview summaries, support ticket themes | Which pain is real vs loud |
| Simulation | How the product behaves at scale | Scenario drafts, edge-case lists | What breaks trust in production |
| Strategic thinking | What to build and refuse | Market scans, competitor matrices | Fit with your strategy and runway |
| Taste | Which good option is right *here* | Option generation (ten prototypes!) | The pick and the explanation |
| Creative execution | What competitors cannot copy | Brainstorm volume | The non-obvious wedge |

Product sense is the umbrella. **Taste** is the skill people name when they cannot explain why one good option beat another.

## Product taste is not a font choice

Design Twitter uses "taste" to mean pretty interfaces. PM taste is uglier and more useful: **trained judgment about what should exist, what should not, and why the gap matters.**

Logan Lincoln's [Product Taste handbook](https://loganlincoln.com/handbook/taste) defines it as choosing the right problem, standard, and trade-off when evidence alone cannot decide. Typography is one expression. So are pricing, onboarding length, error copy, and the support queue you just signed up for.

Saquib J's framing on [product taste as trained judgment](https://saquibj.com/ai-product-management-with-saquib/product-taste-the-one-skill-ai-cannot-replace/) adds a useful distinction: untrained instinct produces faster noise. Trained judgment produces faster edge. Taste comes from consequential choices you lived with, not from vibes you picked up on a podcast.

Shreyas Doshi runs workshops on taste as evaluative skill: can you explain *why* this version fits this product, this user, this moment? If the answer is "the model said so," you have not finished thinking.

## The approve button is the new trap

AI creates a polite failure mode Lincoln names in his handbook: the model proposes options, frames trade-offs, recommends one, and drafts the rationale. You click approve and add no independent thought. The slide looks great. The decision is hollow.

**Good PM + AI:** draft five spec variants, then you pick one and defend it with customer quotes. Synthesize fifty interview notes into themes, then you run three follow-up calls to stress-test them. Generate twenty prototype directions, then you kill nineteen by lunch.

**Bad PM + AI:** paste the model's prioritization into a deck and call it strategy. Ship "AI-powered" because the board asked. Let the model write the hypothesis so you never formed one.

Rules that keep you honest:

1. **Form a view before you prompt.** Even a rough one. AI should attack your view, not replace it.
2. **Explain the decision in your words.** If you cannot, you have not decided.
3. **Validate model-sourced insight with contact.** One user conversation beats ten elegant summaries.

The job is still success on the scoreboard. AI makes it easier to look busy on the way to the wrong scoreboard.

## Taste at speed

Aakash Gupta's [Taste at speed](https://www.news.aakashg.com/p/taste-at-speed) names a rhythm that did not exist when Horowitz wrote his Netscape memo: when prototypes are cheap, the PM skill is evaluating **working software** fast, killing most of it, shipping the survivors.

Fifteen prototype evaluations a week builds judgment faster than one spec review a month. That does not mean skipping the hypothesis from lesson 1. It means the hypothesis gets tested in clickable form earlier.

Engineers learning PM: you already debug systems. Taste is debugging **desirability** the same way. PMs learning tech: you do not need to out-code the team. You need to out-care about whether the thing should exist.

## AI-native products need a different PM muscle

Everything above applies when **you** use AI to do PM work. A separate problem appears when the **product** is AI: chatbots, copilots, agents, generated content.

Models are probabilistic. They hallucinate, drift, and fail in corners demos skip. Marily Nika, in [Building AI product sense, part 2](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/building-ai-product-sense-part-2) on Lenny's Newsletter, treats this as core PM literacy:

1. **Map failure modes.** Where does the model break? Off-topic questions, edge inputs, ambiguous intent?
2. **Define minimum viable quality (MVQ).** What is good enough to ship, not perfect in a lab?
3. **Design guardrails.** Fallbacks, human handoff, refusal rules, logging for review.

You need to tell a product bug from a model limitation. Users will not. Support will not. Your job is to know before the demo hits Twitter.

## Exposure hours beat dashboard cosplay

Lenny's writing on AI-era PMs keeps returning to the same antidote: **exposure hours**. Talk to users. Read support tickets. Sit on a sales call. Watch someone fail at your onboarding.

Judgment is downstream of contact with reality. A team that has not spoken to a customer in months will run its entire understanding on model summaries. The summaries will be fluent. They will still be wrong.

One SaaS founder puts every new hire on customer support for week one. Harsh? Maybe. The logic is simple: layers between builders and users make it easy to ship the wrong thing beautifully. AI adds another layer unless you deliberately stay close to the front line.

Your job in the AI era is not to type faster. It is to **decide better** and **prove you decided** with evidence the model did not invent.

## What changes on the scoreboard

AI features tempt teams into output thinking. "We need AI" is not a success metric. Tie the bet to the scoreboard from lesson 1:

| Company need | AI bet might move… | Kill signal in ~30 days |
|---|---|---|
| Retention | Time saved on a repeat task, fewer drop-offs after step 2 | Usage of the AI feature flat; core metric unchanged |
| Trust | Faster accurate answers, fewer escalations | Support volume up; CSAT down on AI-touched flows |
| Growth | Lower friction to first value | Signups up, activation flat |
| Cost | Less human handling per ticket | Cost down but quality crashes; rework up |

If you cannot fill the kill signal column, you are shipping a demo, not running a product bet.

## Good PM vs bad PM in the AI era

| Good PM habit | Bad PM habit |
|---|---|
| Uses AI to widen insight, then validates with users | Delegates the why to the model and keeps busywork |
| Can kill a cool demo that fails the hypothesis | Ships "AI-powered" because the slide needed a bullet |
| Owns the scoreboard outcome, including misses | Blames the model, the data, or "the market" |
| Forms a view before prompting | Prompts first, decides never |

Horowitz's original split still applies: tasks vs job. The tasks got faster. The job did not shrink.

## Exercise

**Decision drill (15 minutes).** Pick one product decision you face this week. Before opening any AI tool, write:

1. What success means on the scoreboard (one metric)
2. Your current leaning and why
3. What would change your mind

Then use AI to **challenge** line 2. Not to replace it. After the session, rewrite line 2 in your own words. If it did not move, good. If it moved, you learned something. If you cannot tell the difference, you rubber-stamped.

**Friction log (ongoing).** For one week, note every moment a user (or you, dogfooding) hits confusion, distrust, or workaround in your product. One line per hit. Patterns in that log are taste training. Models will not write the log for you.

## Check

Finish this sentence with specifics:

> The scoreboard metric is ___. AI might help by ___. I will know I was wrong if ___. I will validate with ___ (a human or behavior), not only a model output.
